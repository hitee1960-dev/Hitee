# Part Three: Living Well While Getting Dry {.unnumbered}

# Sleepovers, Camps, Travel, and Setbacks

Bedwetting should never be the reason a child misses out. With planning, almost every child can attend sleepovers, camps, and trips — and doing so is important for their confidence and friendships.

## The sleepover plan

**Before the sleepover**

1. **Talk with your child** about whether they want to go and what would help them feel confident.
2. **Choose the right protection:**
   - Discreet absorbent pants (slim, age-appropriate designs look like underwear).
   - A sleeping bag with a waterproof liner or an absorbent pad tucked inside.
   - Dark-coloured pyjamas.
3. **Consider desmopressin** if prescribed — try it at home first, ideally 2–3 weeks before, to check it works and the dose is right.
4. **Decide whether to tell the host parent.** For younger children, a quiet word with the host parent usually helps — most are understanding, and many have dealt with it themselves. Ask them to be discreet. For older children, respect their wishes.
5. **Pack a discreet kit:** night pants in a toiletries bag or pencil case, a plastic bag for used items, wipes, spare underwear.
6. **Plan the routine:** change into night pants in the bathroom; go to the toilet before sleeping; set a phone vibration alarm for a middle-of-the-night toilet trip if it helps.
7. **Rehearse an exit line:** "I spilled my drink" if anything gets wet.

**On the night**

- Drink well earlier in the day; limit drinks in the evening (strictly, if taking desmopressin). Watch the sleepover snacks — fizzy drinks and cola are common.
- Go to the toilet before bed.

**Afterwards**

- Praise your child for going, whatever happened.
- If there was an accident, treat it as a normal hiccup and plan together for next time.

**Start small:** a sleepover at a grandparent's or cousin's house, or a "late-over" (staying until bedtime and coming home), builds confidence.

## Camps and residential school trips

- **Contact the trip leader or camp nurse confidentially** at least 2–3 weeks before. Experienced staff manage bedwetting regularly and discreetly.
- **Agree a plan with staff**, which might include:
  - a staff member quietly waking your child for a toilet trip before staff go to bed;
  - giving desmopressin at bedtime (supply the medication with clear written instructions);
  - a discreet place to change and store supplies;
  - handling wet items quietly in a sealed bag;
  - a bottom bunk (easier and quicker to get to the toilet).
- **Trial desmopressin at home** for several weeks before. Discuss timing and dose with your doctor.
- **Pack:** absorbent pants, sleeping bag liner, plastic bags, wipes, dark pyjamas, extra underwear.
- **Reassure your child** that staff are on their side.

## Family holidays and travel

- **Pack a travel kit:** portable waterproof mattress protector or travel bed pads, absorbent pants, wipes, bags, spare pyjamas.
- **Carry medication** in hand luggage with a copy of the prescription.
- **Plan for heat:** hot climates increase thirst — while taking desmopressin, be careful about large evening drinks; discuss with your doctor.
- **Alarm treatment and holidays:** you can pause alarm treatment during a short holiday and restart afterwards. For a long trip, consider postponing the start of an alarm program until you're home.
- **Find the toilet** in a new place before bedtime and leave a light on.

## Handling setbacks and relapse

Progress is rarely a straight line. Wet nights can return during:

- illness, especially infections or anything causing fever;
- stress — exams, moving house, family changes, bullying;
- changes in routine — holidays, late nights, extra drinks, parties;
- growth spurts;
- stopping an alarm or medication.

**What to do:**

1. **Stay calm.** A setback is not failure. Tell your child: "This happens. Your body still knows how to do it — we'll get back on track."
2. **Check the basics:** drinks, caffeine, toilet habits, constipation, sleep.
3. **Look for triggers** and red flags. If wetting returns after six months of dryness, or comes with other symptoms, see a doctor.
4. **Restart treatment:** if the alarm worked before, restarting it usually works faster the second time. If desmopressin was stopped, your doctor may restart it and plan a more gradual reduction.
5. **Review with your doctor** if relapses keep happening.

# Emotional Wellbeing, Family Life, and Communication

Bedwetting is a physical condition — but it is lived emotionally, by the child and the whole family.

## How bedwetting can affect a child

Children and teenagers who wet the bed commonly describe:

- embarrassment and shame;
- fear of others finding out;
- avoiding sleepovers, camps, and trips;
- feeling "different" or "babyish";
- worrying at bedtime;
- frustration and disappointment after wet nights;
- in some cases, teasing or bullying.

Research suggests bedwetting can affect self-esteem, particularly in older children — and that **successful treatment improves self-esteem**. This is one of the strongest reasons to treat rather than wait.

## The parent's emotional load

Parents' feelings matter too. Night-time disruption, extra laundry, and worry are exhausting. It's normal to feel frustrated — but it's important that frustration is never directed at the child. Parental intolerance of wetting is known to be associated with poorer treatment outcomes and can harm the child emotionally.

**Look after yourself:**

- Share the night-time duties with a partner or other adult where possible.
- Simplify the work (double-made beds, night kits, absorbent pads).
- Remind yourself: your child is not doing this on purpose — and it will get better.
- If you're feeling overwhelmed, tell your doctor. NICE guidance specifically recognises that parental stress can affect which treatment is suitable, and support is available.

## What to say (and not say)

| Instead of... | Try... |
|---|---|
| "Not again!" | "No problem, let's get you changed." |
| "You're too old for this." | "Lots of kids your age deal with this. We'll figure it out together." |
| "Why can't you just wake up?" | "Your brain is still learning to wake up to your bladder. We're going to help it learn." |
| "If you're dry, you'll get a toy." | "If you drink your water and go to the toilet before bed, you get a star." |
| "Your brother was dry at 4." | "Every body develops on its own timetable." |
| "Don't tell anyone." | "This is private. You get to decide who knows." |

## Siblings

- **Zero tolerance for teasing.** Make this an explicit family rule.
- **Explain it to siblings** in simple terms: "It's something their body is still learning. It's not their fault."
- **Protect privacy** — siblings shouldn't share it with friends.
- **Be fair**: rewards for the child who wets should be about effort, and siblings can have their own chart for something they're working on.
- **Shared bedrooms**: consider a vibrating alarm or a wireless alarm with the receiver in a parent's room.

## Bullying

If your child is being teased or bullied about bedwetting:

1. Listen and reassure them it's not their fault.
2. Contact the school; most schools have anti-bullying policies and will act discreetly.
3. Help your child plan responses (e.g., walking away, telling an adult).
4. Consider counselling support if it's affecting their wellbeing.

## When to seek psychological support

Consider a referral to a counsellor, psychologist, or child mental health service if your child:

- shows persistent low mood, anxiety, or withdrawal;
- avoids school or social activities;
- shows signs of trauma or significant stress (especially if wetting started suddenly after a difficult event);
- has behaviour difficulties that make treatment hard;
- expresses thoughts of self-harm (seek help urgently).

Psychological support works best *alongside* medical treatment, not instead of it.

# Special Situations and Practical Care

## Children with ADHD, autism, or developmental differences

Bedwetting is more common in children with ADHD and autism, and in children with developmental delay. They can still be treated successfully, but approaches may need adapting:

- **ADHD:** Children may forget to drink or go to the toilet during the day, and may find alarm routines harder to follow. Use visual timers, phone reminders, and very simple routines. Some ADHD medications or their timing may affect sleep and appetite — discuss with your doctor. Constipation is also more common.
- **Autism:** Sensory sensitivities can make wearable alarms, new pyjamas, or certain bedding uncomfortable. Mat alarms or vibrating alarms may suit better. Use visual schedules and social stories to explain the plan. Introduce changes gradually. Watch for toilet avoidance due to sensory issues (noisy hand dryers, unfamiliar toilets).
- **Learning disabilities and developmental delay:** Readiness depends on developmental age as much as actual age. Specialist continence services can provide tailored toileting programs.

In all cases, ask your doctor about referral to a specialist continence service experienced in neurodevelopmental conditions.

## Children with diabetes, kidney conditions, or other medical conditions

- **Diabetes:** new or worsening wetting in a child with diabetes may signal high blood sugar — check blood glucose and contact your diabetes team.
- **Kidney or urological conditions, spina bifida, or other neurological conditions:** bedwetting management should be led by the specialist team.
- **Sickle cell disease:** these children often produce more urine and may wet more often; their care team can advise.
- **Sleep apnea:** treating obstructed breathing (sometimes by removing enlarged tonsils and adenoids) can improve or resolve bedwetting.

## When stress or trauma may be involved

Sudden-onset (secondary) wetting can follow stressful events such as bereavement, family breakdown, bullying, or moving home. Rarely, it may be a sign of abuse. If your child's wetting started suddenly together with other changes — fearfulness, withdrawal, sexualised behaviour, unexplained injuries, or reluctance to be with a particular person — talk to your doctor or child protection services. Professionals are trained to handle these concerns sensitively.

## Practical care: cleaning up and skin care

**Skin care**

- Wash or shower your child each morning after a wet night to prevent rashes and odour.
- Use mild, fragrance-free soap and pat dry.
- A barrier cream can help if skin becomes red or sore.
- See a doctor if a rash doesn't improve or if there's pain.

**Laundry tips**

- **Rinse urine-soaked items in cold water** first, then wash as normal. Hot water can set urine odour and stains.
- **Add white vinegar** (about half a cup / 120 mL) to the rinse cycle or **bicarbonate of soda / baking soda** to the wash to neutralise odour. Enzyme-based laundry products also help.
- **Dry in sunlight** where possible — it's a natural deodoriser and stain remover.
- **Have at least three sets** of sheets and mattress protectors in rotation.
- **Use a lidded laundry bin** to contain odour.

**Mattress care**

- Always use a waterproof mattress protector.
- If a mattress gets wet: blot (don't rub) as much as possible, sprinkle with baking soda, leave for several hours, then vacuum. An enzyme cleaner can help with odour. Allow to dry completely.

**Absorbent products**

- Choose the right size — products that are too big or too small leak.
- Try a few types: disposable pull-ups, washable/reusable night pants, and bed pads.
- For older children and teens, choose discreet youth/adult-sized designs.
- Remember that for alarm treatment, absorbent pants usually need to be removed.

## Keeping costs down

- Ask your doctor whether **alarms can be loaned** through a local continence service or health system.
- Washable bed pads and night pants can be more economical than disposables over time.
- Check whether your health insurance or national health service covers alarms, medication, or continence products.

## Final words: Keep going

Bedwetting can feel like it will last forever. It won't. Nearly every child eventually becomes dry, and with the right approach, most can get there much sooner than by waiting alone.

Remember the four pillars of this book:

1. **Understand** — it's physical, common, and nobody's fault.
2. **Build the foundations** — good drinking, regular toileting, healthy bowels, and healthy sleep.
3. **Treat actively** — alarm, desmopressin, or both, with your doctor's support, at the right age for your child.
4. **Protect confidence** — kindness, privacy, planning, and never letting wet nights stop your child from living fully.

Your patience, calm, and belief in your child are the most powerful tools in this book. Dry nights are ahead.
