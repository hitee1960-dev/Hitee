# Part One: Understanding Bedwetting {.unnumbered}

# Understanding Bedwetting: What It Is and Why It Happens

## What counts as "bedwetting"?

Doctors use the term **nocturnal enuresis** for wetting during sleep in a child aged five or older. Before five, night-time wetting is a normal part of development and is not considered a medical problem at all.

There are a few important subtypes. Knowing which one your child has helps you choose the right approach.

| Type | What it means | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary enuresis** | The child has never been reliably dry at night for six months or more. | The most common type (about 75–80% of cases). Usually a developmental issue that responds well to standard treatment. |
| **Secondary enuresis** | Wetting returns after the child has been dry for at least six months. | Needs a doctor's check, because a new cause (infection, constipation, diabetes, stress, sleep problems) may be behind it. |
| **Monosymptomatic enuresis** | Night wetting only, with no daytime bladder symptoms. | Usually responds to alarm or desmopressin. |
| **Non-monosymptomatic enuresis** | Night wetting *plus* daytime symptoms — urgency, frequent peeing, daytime accidents, holding manoeuvres, weak stream. | The daytime bladder problem should be treated first or alongside; a doctor's assessment is recommended. |

## How common is it?

Bedwetting is one of the most common childhood conditions. Figures vary between studies and countries, but a consistent picture emerges:

| Age | Approximate share of children who wet the bed |
|---|---|
| 5 years | about 15–20% (1 in 5 to 1 in 7) |
| 7 years | about 7–10% |
| 10 years | about 5% |
| 15 years | about 1–3% |
| Adults | about 0.5–2% |

Boys are affected more often than girls in childhood (roughly twice as often), though the gap narrows in the teen years.

Without treatment, roughly **14–15% of children who wet the bed become dry each year on their own**. That sounds encouraging — and it is — but it also means that in any given year, about 85% of children who wet will still be wetting next year. Research also suggests that the more often a child wets (for example, every night rather than once a week) and the older they are, the less likely it is to clear up by itself. That is why older children, and those who wet most nights, benefit most from active treatment.

## The three main causes

Almost all bedwetting comes down to a mismatch between three things. Think of it as a simple equation:

> **Bedwetting happens when the amount of urine made overnight is more than the bladder can hold — and the child doesn't wake up to go.**

### 1. Too much urine at night (nocturnal polyuria)

Normally, the brain releases more of a hormone called **vasopressin** (also called antidiuretic hormone, or ADH) at night. Vasopressin tells the kidneys to make less, more concentrated urine while we sleep. In many children who wet the bed, this night-time rise in vasopressin is smaller or delayed, so the kidneys keep producing daytime amounts of urine all night. This is the type of bedwetting that responds especially well to the medication **desmopressin**, which is a man-made version of vasopressin.

Large evening drinks, caffeine, fizzy drinks, and salty or high-protein late meals can also increase night-time urine.

### 2. A bladder that holds less at night (reduced bladder capacity or overactivity)

Some children have a bladder that holds less than expected for their age, or that contracts ("squeezes") before it is full. These children often also show daytime signs: needing to go urgently, going very often, little daytime accidents, or doing the "potty dance" — crossing legs, squatting, or pressing on their crotch. Constipation frequently makes this worse (see below). This type responds especially well to the **bedwetting alarm**, to good daytime drinking and toileting habits, and to treating constipation.

### 3. Not waking up to the bladder's signal (arousal)

Most children who wet the bed are not unusually "deep" sleepers in the ordinary sense, but their brain does not respond to the signal of a full bladder by waking them up. This is a maturation issue in the connection between bladder and brain. It is not something a child can control by willpower. The bedwetting alarm works largely by training this connection.

## Other factors that contribute

**Genetics.** Bedwetting runs strongly in families. Classic studies estimate that if neither parent wet the bed as a child, the risk is about 15%. If one parent did, the risk rises to about 44%; if both did, to about 77%. Many parents only discover that they — or a grandparent, aunt, or uncle — wet the bed as a child when they ask. Sharing this with your child can be enormously reassuring.

**Constipation.** This is one of the most overlooked causes. A rectum full of stool presses on the bladder, reducing how much it can hold and irritating it so it contracts unexpectedly. Constipation is much more common in children who wet the bed than in other children, and studies show that children who are constipated respond less well to bedwetting treatments until the constipation is treated. Importantly, a child can be constipated *even if they poo every day* — if the stools are hard, small, painful, or very large and infrequent. See Chapter 2 for how to spot and manage it.

**Sleep-disordered breathing.** Loud snoring, pauses in breathing, mouth breathing, and restless sleep can be signs of obstructed breathing during sleep, often from large tonsils or adenoids. This can increase night-time urine production and disrupt normal waking. In some children, treating the airway problem resolves the bedwetting.

**Neurodevelopmental conditions.** Children with ADHD or autism have higher rates of bedwetting. They can still be treated successfully, though approaches may need adapting (see Chapter 12).

**Stress and life events.** Stress does not usually *cause* primary bedwetting, but it can trigger secondary bedwetting (a return of wetting after being dry) — for example after a new sibling, a house move, starting school, parental separation, bereavement, or bullying. Bedwetting itself also causes stress, which can create a vicious cycle.

**Medical conditions (uncommon).** A small number of cases are caused by urinary tract infection, diabetes (type 1 or type 2), kidney problems, structural problems of the urinary tract, or nerve problems affecting the spine. These usually come with other warning signs, listed in Chapter 3.

## What does *not* cause bedwetting

It is worth saying clearly, because these myths still circulate:

- It is **not** laziness, attention-seeking, or bad behaviour.
- It is **not** caused by bad parenting.
- It is **not**, in most cases, a sign of emotional disturbance or trauma.
- It is **not** something a child can stop by trying harder or being punished.

## Bedwetting myths and facts

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| "They'll definitely grow out of it." | Most will, eventually — but about 1–2% of adults still wet. Waiting years is not necessary when effective treatments exist. |
| "Cutting out all drinks after lunch will fix it." | Restricting fluids too much can *reduce* bladder capacity and cause constipation. Children need to drink well during the day; only the last hour before bed should be limited. |
| "Waking them at night to pee will train them." | "Lifting" or waking at set times can reduce wet beds in the short term but does not teach long-term dryness (NICE guidance). It's a practical measure, not a cure. |
| "Punishing or shaming will motivate them." | Punishment makes bedwetting worse and harms self-esteem. It should never be used. |
| "Pull-ups make bedwetting last longer." | Absorbent pants don't cause bedwetting. For children starting an alarm program, though, they are usually removed so the alarm can detect wetting. |
| "Bedwetting means something is psychologically wrong." | In most children it is a physical, developmental issue. Emotional difficulties are more often a *result* of bedwetting than a cause. |

## The good news: it can be treated

Today's main treatments are effective for most children:

- **Bedwetting alarms** help around two-thirds or more of children who use them consistently, and they have lower relapse rates than medication once treatment ends.
- **Desmopressin** reduces wetting in most children while they take it and works quickly — often within days — making it ideal for sleepovers and camps.
- **Basic measures** — good daytime drinking, regular toilet visits, treating constipation, and positive encouragement — help many children on their own and make the other treatments work better.

The next chapter explains each of these tools in detail.

# The Bedwetting Toolkit: Proven Strategies and Treatments

This chapter describes the full set of tools available. The age-group chapters in Part Two tell you *which* tools to use, *when*, and *how* for each stage. Come back to this chapter whenever you need the details.

## Tool 1: Healthy drinking habits

Children who wet the bed often drink too little during the day and too much in the evening — sometimes because they (or their parents) are trying to avoid wetting. This pattern makes things worse. The goal is to **drink well and evenly during the day, and taper off before bed**.

**Daily fluid guide (water-based drinks, approximate).** These ranges are based on NICE guidance and vary with weather, activity, and diet:

| Age | Girls | Boys |
|---|---|---|
| 4–8 years | 1,000–1,400 mL (about 4–6 cups) | 1,000–1,400 mL (about 4–6 cups) |
| 9–13 years | 1,200–2,100 mL (about 5–9 cups) | 1,400–2,300 mL (about 6–10 cups) |
| 14–18 years | 1,400–2,500 mL (about 6–10 cups) | 2,100–3,200 mL (about 9–13 cups) |

*(One cup ≈ 240 mL / 8 fl oz.)*

**How to spread drinks across the day:**

- Aim for **6–8 drinks spread through the day**: on waking, with breakfast, mid-morning, lunch, mid-afternoon, after school, and with the evening meal.
- Try to have **most of the day's fluid before about 5 p.m.** — a full water bottle at school helps enormously. Check that your child's school allows easy access to water and the toilet.
- Allow a small drink with the evening meal, then **stop drinks about one hour before bed** (provided they've drunk well during the day).
- **Avoid caffeine** — cola, energy drinks, tea, coffee, and chocolate drinks — especially in the afternoon and evening. Caffeine increases urine production and irritates the bladder.
- **Limit fizzy (carbonated) drinks and sugary drinks**, which can also irritate the bladder.
- Water is best. Milk is fine at meals.
- Be mindful of **salty, high-protein late snacks**, which can increase night-time urine.

## Tool 2: Regular toilet habits

- **Daytime:** Encourage your child to go to the toilet regularly — about **every 2–3 hours, roughly 4–7 times a day** — rather than holding on until the last minute.
- **Before bed:** Use the "double void": go to the toilet as part of the bedtime routine, then go again just before lights out.
- **Posture matters:** Children should sit comfortably with feet supported (use a footstool if their feet don't touch the floor) and knees slightly apart, relaxed, and take their time. Boys who tend to rush may benefit from sitting to pee in the evening to empty fully.
- **Make night-time access easy:** Night-lights in the hallway and bathroom, a clear path, and — if needed — a potty or portable urinal in the bedroom.

## Tool 3: Treating constipation

Because constipation is so closely linked to bedwetting, checking for it is one of the most valuable things you can do.

**Signs your child may be constipated:**

- Fewer than three poos a week, **or**
- Hard, lumpy, or pellet-like stools (types 1–2 on the Bristol Stool Chart), **or**
- Straining, pain, or bleeding when pooing, **or**
- Very large stools that may block the toilet, **or**
- Soiling or skid-marks in underwear, **or**
- Tummy pain, poor appetite, or avoiding the toilet.

**What helps:**

- Plenty of fluids (see Tool 1) and fibre — fruit, vegetables, whole grains, beans, lentils.
- Regular, relaxed toilet sits for 5–10 minutes after meals (when the natural gut reflex is strongest), with feet supported on a stool.
- Daily physical activity.
- If constipation persists, **see a doctor**. Doctors commonly recommend an osmotic laxative (such as polyethylene glycol / macrogol), often for weeks or months. Don't stop laxatives suddenly once things improve — follow your doctor's plan.

Treating constipation can resolve bedwetting entirely in some children and makes alarms and medication work better in others.

## Tool 4: Positive reinforcement and reward systems

Rewards can help motivate children — but only if they reward things the child can control. **Never reward dry nights alone**, because a child can't control those directly, and failing to earn the reward feels like punishment.

**Do reward:**

- Drinking the recommended amount during the day.
- Going to the toilet before bed (and doing the "double void").
- Getting up to use the toilet at night.
- Waking up to the alarm and helping to reset it.
- Helping to change the sheets without fuss.

**How:**

- Use a simple chart with stickers or stars (see Appendix B).
- Agree small, frequent rewards (a sticker, extra story, choosing dinner) and occasional bigger ones for a run of stars.
- Praise specifically: "You got up to the toilet all by yourself — that's brilliant."
- Keep it fun. If the chart becomes stressful, stop and try again later.

Reward systems on their own suit younger children (roughly 5–7 years) with infrequent wetting. For older children and frequent wetting, they work best combined with an alarm or medication.

## Tool 5: The bedwetting alarm

The bedwetting alarm is the most effective long-term treatment for most children and is recommended as **first-line treatment** by both NICE and the International Children's Continence Society (ICCS) for motivated families.

### How it works

A small moisture sensor (in the underwear, on a pad under the sheet, or in a clip) detects the first drops of urine and triggers an alarm (sound, vibration, light, or a combination). At first, the alarm wakes the child (or more often, the parent) *after* wetting has started. Over weeks, the brain learns to link the feeling of a full bladder with waking up — and eventually, many children start either waking before they wet, or sleeping through the night without needing to go, because the bladder learns to "hold on."

### Types of alarm

| Type | How it works | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| **Body-worn (wearable)** | Sensor clips to underwear; alarm unit clips to pyjamas near the shoulder. | Most children; detects wetting fastest. |
| **Bell-and-pad (mat)** | Sensor mat goes under the sheet; alarm sits beside the bed. | Children who dislike anything attached to them; heavy sleepers. |
| **Wireless** | Sensor sends a signal to a receiver in the child's room and/or the parent's room. | Families where a parent needs to hear the alarm from another room. |
| **Vibrating** | Vibrates instead of / as well as sounds. | Children with hearing impairment; teens sharing rooms; dormitories. |

Bedwetting alarms are available to buy and, in some countries, may be loaned through health services or continence clinics — ask your doctor or nurse.

### How to use an alarm successfully

1. **Choose the right time.** Start when your family has a stable few months — not during exams, a house move, a new baby, or holidays.
2. **Commit to nightly use.** The alarm must be used *every night*. Inconsistent use is the most common reason alarms fail.
3. **Explain it to your child** positively, as a training tool — like training wheels for the brain.
4. **Practise during the day.** Have your child lie in bed, "pretend" the alarm sounds (trigger it with a damp cloth), and act out getting up, switching it off, going to the toilet, and coming back. Do this a few times.
5. **No absorbent pants** — the sensor must detect wetness. Use a waterproof mattress protector.
6. **When the alarm goes off:**
   - The child (with your help at first) should wake up fully, switch off the alarm, and go to the toilet to finish peeing.
   - Change wet clothes and bedding together, calmly. Keep spare pyjamas and a towel ready.
   - Reset the alarm before going back to sleep.
   - Many children don't wake to the alarm at first. **A parent needs to wake the child** — calmly, using their name, sitting them up, until they are truly awake. This is normal and usually needed for the first few weeks.
7. **Keep a record** of wet and dry nights and the size of the wet patch (see Appendix C). Shrinking wet patches and the alarm going off later in the night are early signs of progress.
8. **Give it time.** Assess progress after about **4 weeks**. NICE suggests continuing if there are signs of improvement, and it often takes **6–16 weeks** to achieve dryness. In one large study, the average time to dryness was about two months.
9. **Know when it's done.** Continue until your child has achieved **14 consecutive dry nights**.
10. **Optional — "overlearning" to prevent relapse:** After 14 dry nights, some clinicians suggest giving an extra drink (about 200–500 mL, depending on age) an hour before bed while continuing the alarm, until 14 more consecutive dry nights are achieved. This can strengthen the habit. Discuss with your health professional before trying this.

### What to expect

- Studies report that alarm treatment leads to dryness in roughly **50–80% of children** who use it properly, with one large clinical series reporting 76% success.
- Some children relapse after stopping (estimates range widely). If wetting returns, **restarting the alarm** usually works faster the second time.
- Alarms are disruptive for the whole household at first. Planning, team spirit, and a sense of humour help.

### When an alarm may not suit your family

NICE notes an alarm may be unsuitable if wetting is very infrequent (fewer than 1–2 times a week), if parents are under significant stress or are struggling emotionally with the bedwetting, or if the child or family is not motivated. In those cases, desmopressin is usually the alternative.

## Tool 6: Desmopressin (medication)

**Desmopressin** is a synthetic version of the body's natural antidiuretic hormone (vasopressin). Taken at bedtime, it reduces the amount of urine the kidneys produce overnight.

- It is a **first-line treatment** alongside the alarm (ICCS, NICE), and is especially useful when:
  - fast or short-term improvement is the priority (sleepovers, camps, school trips);
  - the alarm isn't suitable or hasn't worked;
  - the child produces large volumes of urine at night (nocturnal polyuria).
- It is available as **tablets** or **melt-in-the-mouth tablets (melts)**, prescribed by a doctor. It is generally used in children aged 5 and over.
- It works quickly — often within the first few nights — and helps the majority of children reduce wetting, with a good proportion becoming completely dry while taking it.
- It does **not cure** the underlying cause. Many children start wetting again when it is stopped, so doctors often plan structured breaks (e.g., a week off every three months) to check whether it is still needed, and may reduce the dose gradually rather than stopping suddenly.

### Essential safety rules for desmopressin

Desmopressin is safe when used correctly, but **drinking too much while taking it can cause a dangerous drop in blood sodium (hyponatremia)**, which in rare cases causes seizures.

- **Limit drinks from 1 hour before taking the dose until 8 hours afterward** — only small sips if thirsty. Follow your doctor's exact instructions.
- **Stop taking it during any illness that causes vomiting, diarrhoea, or fever,** or whenever your child may need to drink a lot (e.g., hot weather or heavy exercise) — and restart only when well.
- **Seek medical advice immediately** if your child has a headache, nausea, vomiting, confusion, or unusual drowsiness while taking it.
- The **nasal spray** form is **no longer approved for bedwetting** in the US and UK because of a higher risk of low sodium. Tablets or melts are used instead.
- Always use the dose prescribed and never give extra.

## Tool 7: Combination and specialist treatments

If the alarm or desmopressin alone doesn't work, a doctor or specialist continence clinic may suggest:

- **Alarm plus desmopressin** together.
- **Anticholinergic medications** (e.g., oxybutynin), which calm an overactive bladder, usually combined with desmopressin, especially when there are daytime symptoms. Constipation must be managed first because these medicines can worsen it.
- **Imipramine** (a tricyclic medication) — a specialist-only option used rarely because of potential side effects and danger in overdose.
- **Bladder training programs, pelvic floor work, or urotherapy** with a specialist nurse or physiotherapist, especially for children with daytime symptoms.
- **Investigation of the airway** (ear, nose and throat assessment) if snoring and breathing pauses suggest sleep-disordered breathing.
- **Psychological support** where bedwetting is causing significant distress, low self-esteem, or behaviour problems, or where stress is a trigger.

## Tool 8: Practical protection (managing, not treating)

These don't cure bedwetting, but they make life much easier and protect dignity:

- **Waterproof mattress protector** and pillow protector (breathable, quiet types are best for comfort).
- **Layering the bed** ("double-making"): protector – sheet – protector – sheet. If the bed gets wet at night, strip the top two layers and there's a dry bed underneath — no fumbling at 2 a.m.
- **Absorbent bed pads** placed on top of the sheet.
- **Absorbent pants / pull-ups / disposable or washable night pants** in age-appropriate sizes, for children not on an alarm program, for travel, or for sleepovers. Discreet teen and young-adult versions exist.
- **A "night kit"** by the bed: clean pyjamas, underwear, wipes, a towel, and a bag for wet items, so your child can change independently.

## Things that **don't** work (or are not recommended)

- **Punishment, shaming, or teasing** — harmful and makes wetting worse.
- **Severe fluid restriction** throughout the day.
- **"Lifting" or waking as a long-term strategy** — fine as a short-term practical measure, but it doesn't build long-term dryness.
- **Unproven products** promising "instant cures," herbal remedies, or devices without evidence. Some complementary approaches (such as acupuncture or hypnotherapy) have limited evidence and are not recommended as standard care; if you want to try them, discuss them with your doctor and don't let them replace proven treatment.

# When to See a Doctor: Red Flags and What to Expect

## Routine reasons to see a doctor

You should talk to a doctor, pediatrician, or nurse about bedwetting if:

- Your child is **5 years or older** and still wetting the bed regularly and it is bothering your child or your family.
- Your child is **7 or older** and still wetting — treatment is generally recommended by this age.
- Your child has **daytime symptoms** too: daytime accidents, urgency, going very often, or holding manoeuvres.
- Your child seems **constipated** or has soiling.
- The bedwetting is causing **distress**, low self-esteem, bullying, or is stopping your child from joining in activities.
- You have tried basic measures for a few months without improvement.

You do *not* need to wait for a certain age if your child is upset by it. NICE guidance specifically states that children under 5 should not be excluded from management if their bedwetting is a concern.

## Red flags: see a doctor promptly

Seek medical advice **soon** (within days) if bedwetting comes with any of these:

- **Wetting that starts again after six months or more of being dry** (secondary enuresis), especially if sudden.
- **Excessive thirst, drinking much more than usual, needing to pee much more often, weight loss, or tiredness** — these can be signs of **diabetes**, which needs urgent testing.
- **Pain, burning, or stinging when peeing, cloudy or smelly urine, fever, or blood in the urine** — possible **urinary tract infection**.
- **A weak or dribbling urine stream, straining to pee**, or being constantly damp.
- **Loud snoring, gasping, or pauses in breathing during sleep**.
- **Leg weakness, changes in walking, back pain, or a dimple, tuft of hair, or lump at the base of the spine** — possible nerve problems.
- **Soiling, severe constipation, or tummy swelling**.
- **Any concern about abuse or significant emotional trauma** — sudden-onset wetting together with changes in behaviour should always be taken seriously.
- **Wetting in a teenager or young adult that is new or getting worse.**

> **Go to an emergency department or call emergency services** if your child is very unwell, drowsy, vomiting, breathing fast, or has severe tummy pain alongside excessive thirst and urination (possible diabetic ketoacidosis), or has a seizure, severe headache, or confusion while taking desmopressin.

## Preparing for the appointment

Doctors find it enormously helpful when families arrive prepared. Before your visit:

1. **Keep a bladder diary for 2 days** (preferably a weekend) — record drinks, times and volumes of urine passed, and any accidents. See Appendix A.
2. **Record wet and dry nights for 2 weeks**, including whether wetting happened once or more, and roughly when.
3. **Note bowel habits** for a week — frequency and stool type.
4. **List any other symptoms**: snoring, daytime wetting, urgency, thirst, behaviour or sleep changes.
5. **Think about family history**: did parents, siblings, or other relatives wet the bed?
6. **Note what you've tried** and what happened.
7. **Write down your questions** (see Appendix E).

## What the doctor will usually do

- **Take a history** — the type of wetting, daytime symptoms, drinking, bowels, sleep, development, family history, and how the family is coping.
- **Do a physical exam** — checking the tummy, the lower back and spine, the genitals, and sometimes the legs and reflexes.
- **Test the urine** (dipstick) — mainly to check for sugar (diabetes) and infection.
- **Most children need no further tests.** Blood tests, ultrasound scans, or specialist referral are only needed if something unusual is found or treatment doesn't work.
- **Agree a plan** — usually basic advice plus an alarm or desmopressin, and a follow-up appointment.

Remember: a good health professional will treat your child's bedwetting with respect, will talk *to* your child (not just about them), and will never blame you or your child.
