# Part Two: Age-by-Age Action Plans {.unnumbered}

# Ages 0–2: Laying the Foundations

## What's normal at this age

At this age, **wetting at night is not bedwetting — it is simply normal**. Babies and toddlers do not have the physical ability to stay dry through the night. Their bladders are small, their bodies have not yet developed the night-time pattern of the hormone that reduces urine production, and the connection between bladder and brain that allows them to wake (or hold on) is still developing.

Most children gain **daytime** bladder control somewhere between about 18 months and 3 years. **Night-time dryness usually comes later** — commonly between 3 and 5 years, and sometimes not until 6 or 7. A small number of toddlers become dry at night early, often at the same time as daytime training, but most do not. Neither is "better."

So there is nothing to *treat* in this age group. But there is a great deal you can do to lay healthy foundations that make night-time dryness easier later — and to avoid creating problems (like constipation or toilet fear) that can make bedwetting more likely and longer-lasting.

## What parents worry about at this age

- **"My 2-year-old is out of diapers in the day — why not at night?"** Day and night control are controlled differently. Daytime control depends on awareness and choice; night-time control depends on hormones, bladder size, and brain maturation during sleep, which no amount of practice can rush.
- **"Should I stop night-time milk or drinks?"** Toddlers still need adequate fluid. Don't restrict drinks to try to achieve dryness at this age.
- **"Should I wake my toddler to pee?"** There's no need, and it can disrupt everyone's sleep.

## The goals for ages 0–2

1. Support **healthy bladder and bowel development**.
2. **Prevent constipation**, which is one of the biggest avoidable contributors to later wetting.
3. Build a **positive, relaxed relationship with the toilet**.
4. Begin **daytime toilet training when your child is ready** — without pressure.
5. **Leave night-time dryness alone** for now.

## Healthy foundations

### Drinks

- Offer water regularly through the day, especially once your toddler is eating solids and in warm weather.
- After 12 months, cow's milk (or an appropriate alternative) is usually offered at meals and snacks — follow your health professional's guidance on amounts. Too much milk can reduce appetite for fibre-rich foods and contribute to constipation.
- Avoid sugary drinks, fizzy drinks, and anything with caffeine (including tea, cola, and chocolate drinks).
- Begin establishing the habit of **drinks with meals and between meals**, rather than lots of fluid in the evening.

### Bowels

- Offer fibre-rich foods: fruits (pears, prunes, plums, berries), vegetables, whole grains, beans, and lentils.
- Watch for **signs of constipation**: hard, pellet-like stools; straining with pain; fewer than three poos a week; blood on the stool or diaper; or a toddler who hides, cries, or "holds on" when they need to poo.
- If your child is constipated, talk to your health visitor, pediatrician, or family doctor — **early treatment prevents a painful cycle of withholding** that can last for years and is linked to wetting.

### Sleep

- A consistent, calming bedtime routine (bath, pyjamas, teeth, story, sleep) supports healthy sleep, which is good for every aspect of development.
- If your toddler **snores loudly most nights**, gasps, or has pauses in breathing, mention it to a doctor — enlarged tonsils and adenoids are common at this age and can affect sleep quality.

## Daytime toilet training: readiness first

Night dryness comes after daytime control, so successful, *relaxed* daytime training is the foundation. Look for **signs of readiness**, which typically appear between about 18 months and 3 years:

- Stays dry for 1–2 hours at a time during the day.
- Shows awareness of wetting or soiling — tells you, pulls at the diaper, hides to poo.
- Shows interest in the toilet or in copying others.
- Can follow simple instructions.
- Can walk to the potty, sit, and get up.
- Can help pull pants up and down.

**If your child is not showing these signs, wait.** Starting too early tends to make training take longer, not shorter.

### How to support daytime training

- **Get the equipment ready:** a potty and/or a toilet seat insert with a footstool, so feet are supported (this helps children relax their pelvic muscles and empty fully).
- **Introduce it playfully:** read toddler books about using the potty, let your child sit fully clothed, let them see family members use the toilet.
- **Build in regular sits:** on waking, after meals, before and after naps, before bath, and before bed. Sitting after meals takes advantage of the body's natural reflex to empty the bowel.
- **Praise effort, not results.** "You sat on the potty — great job!"
- **Expect accidents.** Respond calmly: "Oops, the pee came out. Let's get clean pants. Next time we'll try the potty."
- **Never punish, scold, or show disgust** — this can create toilet fear and withholding.
- **Don't force sitting** or keep a child on the potty for long periods.

## What to do at night

- **Keep using diapers (nappies) or night-time pants at night.** This is not "going backward" — it's appropriate.
- **Don't wake your child to pee** at this age.
- **Don't restrict fluids** to try to achieve dryness.
- **Notice dry diapers.** If your child begins waking with a dry diaper on most mornings for a week or two, they may be ready to try nights without one — usually after their third birthday (see Chapter 5).

## When to talk to a health professional

At this age, consult your doctor or health visitor if:

- Your child shows **signs of constipation** or pain when pooing.
- Your child seems to be **always wet** — constantly dribbling rather than wetting in "batches." This can occasionally signal a structural issue.
- The **urine stream is weak** or your child strains to pee.
- Your child has **fevers without an obvious cause** (possible urinary tract infection — urine should be checked).
- Your child is **unusually thirsty**, drinking and urinating excessively, or losing weight.
- Your toddler **snores loudly** with breathing pauses.
- You are concerned about your child's **development** more generally.

## ✅ Clear Action Plan: Ages 0–2

**Do now**

- [ ] Offer water regularly throughout the day; avoid sugary, fizzy, and caffeinated drinks.
- [ ] Include fibre-rich foods at every meal.
- [ ] Watch for constipation; see a health professional early if you spot it.
- [ ] Keep a calm, consistent bedtime routine.
- [ ] Keep diapers/night pants on at night — night dryness will come later.

**When your child shows readiness signs (often 18 months – 3 years)**

- [ ] Buy a potty or toilet insert and a footstool.
- [ ] Introduce the potty through play and books.
- [ ] Start regular, relaxed potty sits: on waking, after meals, before bath and bed.
- [ ] Praise every attempt; handle accidents calmly.

**Don't**

- ✗ Don't restrict drinks or wake your child at night to try for dryness.
- ✗ Don't punish accidents or force sitting.
- ✗ Don't compare your child with others — the age range for dryness is wide.

**See a professional if:** constipation, constant dribbling, weak stream, unexplained fevers, excessive thirst, or loud snoring with pauses.

# Ages 3–5: The Journey to Dry Nights

## What's normal at this age

This is the age when most children *begin* to become dry at night — but many are not there yet, and that is entirely normal. Around **one in five five-year-olds still wets the bed**. Doctors do not formally diagnose bedwetting until age 5, and many clinicians consider wetting up to age 6 or 7 part of the normal range.

Children in this group often fall into one of three patterns:

1. **Dry during the day and starting to wake up with dry diapers** — likely ready for nights without them.
2. **Dry during the day but consistently wet every morning** — the body isn't ready yet for night dryness.
3. **Still having frequent daytime accidents** — focus on daytime control first.

## The goals for ages 3–5

1. **Consolidate daytime bladder and bowel habits.**
2. **Recognise readiness for night-time dryness** — and try it at the right time.
3. **Protect self-esteem** and keep things low-pressure.
4. **Identify any problems** early (constipation, daytime symptoms, snoring) — and, from age 5, consider whether a doctor's assessment would help.

## Is my child ready for dry nights?

Signs that your child may be ready to go without night diapers or pants:

- **Dry diapers in the morning** on most days for at least a week or two.
- **Dry after daytime naps.**
- Reliably **dry during the day**, with few accidents.
- **Waking in the night** asking to go to the toilet.
- **Wanting** to wear "big kid" underwear at night.

If your child is waking wet every morning, they're probably not ready — keep using night pants and check again every couple of months. There's no harm in waiting.

## How to start night training

1. **Pick a calm time** — avoid starting during illness, a house move, a new sibling's arrival, or starting a new nursery or school.
2. **Talk about it positively.** "Your body is getting so good at holding pee. Shall we try sleeping in pants like a big kid?"
3. **Protect the bed:** waterproof mattress protector and the "double-make" method (protector – sheet – protector – sheet) for easy night changes.
4. **Bedtime routine:** a small drink with dinner, no big drinks in the last hour before bed, and a toilet visit as the last thing before lights out (and a "double void" — go once at the start of the routine, again at the end).
5. **Make night toilet trips easy:** a night-light in the bedroom, hallway, and bathroom; a potty in the bedroom if the toilet is far away. Tell your child, "If you wake up and need to pee, you can go to the potty — or call me and I'll help."
6. **Give it a fair trial** — about **two to four weeks**.
7. **If there are frequent wet nights** (for example, most nights) after a few weeks, it's perfectly okay to **go back to night pants** for a few months and try again. Present it positively: "Your body isn't quite ready yet. We'll try again after your birthday."

## Daytime habits that build night-time dryness

What happens during the day strongly influences what happens at night.

- **Drinks:** about 6–8 cups/drinks spread through the day (around 1,000–1,400 mL for ages 4–8), mostly before late afternoon. Water and milk are best. No caffeine; limit fizzy and sugary drinks.
- **Regular toilet visits:** encourage your child to go every 2–3 hours — on waking, at mid-morning, at lunch, mid-afternoon, after nursery or school, before dinner, and at bedtime. Young children often "forget" because they're busy playing.
- **No holding on:** watch for the "potty dance" (crossing legs, wiggling, squatting on a heel). If you see it, gently suggest a toilet trip. Frequent holding can signal an overactive bladder — mention it to your doctor.
- **Healthy bowels:** fibre, fluids, activity, and relaxed toilet sits after meals with feet supported. Constipation at this age is common and very treatable.
- **Nursery/school:** let staff know your child needs easy access to water and the toilet.

## Handling wet nights

- **Stay calm and matter-of-fact.** "Oops, the pee came out while you were sleeping. Let's get you dry." No sighing, no frustration in your voice.
- **Keep night changes quick and quiet**: dim light, minimal talking, fresh pyjamas, strip the top layer of the double-made bed, back to sleep.
- **Involve your child gently** — e.g., putting wet pyjamas in the laundry basket in the morning — as a normal routine, *never as a punishment*.
- **Wash your child in the morning** (a quick shower or bath, or wipes) to prevent skin irritation and odour.
- **Never punish, shame, or tease**, and don't let siblings tease either.
- **Protect privacy**: don't discuss wet nights in front of others.

## Using rewards at this age

Simple sticker charts can work well for 4- and 5-year-olds — but reward the right things:

- ⭐ Drinking their water bottle during the day.
- ⭐ Going to the toilet before bed.
- ⭐ Getting up at night to use the toilet or potty.
- ⭐ Helping to put sheets in the laundry.

Avoid charts that reward dry nights alone — your child can't control them yet, and missing out feels like failure.

## Should we try "lifting" (waking to pee)?

Some families carry a sleeping child to the toilet before the parents go to bed. This can reduce the number of wet beds and may be a helpful short-term measure for some families, but research and NICE guidance indicate that it **does not teach long-term dryness**. If you use it, try to make sure your child is properly awake while using the toilet, and vary the time. Don't feel you must do it.

## When to see a doctor (ages 3–5)

Talk to your doctor or health professional if:

- Your child is **5 or older** and wetting at night is bothering them or the family.
- Your child has **daytime wetting** after age 4, urgency, very frequent peeing, or holding manoeuvres.
- Your child shows **signs of constipation** or soiling.
- Your child was **dry for six months or more and has started wetting again**.
- Your child **snores loudly** most nights or has breathing pauses.
- There are any **red-flag symptoms**: excessive thirst, weight loss, pain or burning when peeing, blood in urine, fevers, weak stream or constant dampness.

### Treatments at this age

Bedwetting alarms and desmopressin are generally used from **age 5 upward**. Treatment is rarely needed before 5, but NICE guidance makes clear that **children under 5 should not be excluded** if wetting is causing problems — a health professional can advise on what's appropriate. In most children aged 3–5, the focus is on daytime habits, constipation, and patience.

## ✅ Clear Action Plan: Ages 3–5

**Every day**

- [ ] 6–8 drinks spread across the day, mostly before late afternoon; no caffeine.
- [ ] Toilet visits every 2–3 hours and before bed (double void at bedtime).
- [ ] Fibre-rich foods; check poos for signs of constipation.
- [ ] Night-lights on the route to the toilet; potty in the bedroom if needed.

**Deciding on night training**

- [ ] Watch for dry mornings and dry naps for 1–2 weeks.
- [ ] If ready: protect the bed (double-make), talk positively, and try for 2–4 weeks.
- [ ] If mostly wet after 2–4 weeks: return to night pants without fuss and retry in 2–3 months.

**Wet nights**

- [ ] Stay calm and kind; quick, quiet changes.
- [ ] Wash in the morning; protect privacy; no teasing from siblings.
- [ ] Use a sticker chart for *actions* (drinking, toilet before bed, getting up), not for dry nights.

**Book a doctor's visit if:**

- [ ] Your child is 5+ and wetting is bothering them or you.
- [ ] Daytime symptoms, constipation, snoring, or any red flags (see Chapter 3).
- [ ] Wetting returned after 6+ months dry.

# Ages 6–8: Building Skills and Confidence

## What's happening at this age

Between 6 and 8, roughly **7–10% of children still wet the bed** — in an average classroom of 30, that's two or three children. At this age bedwetting is now considered a medical condition (nocturnal enuresis) that **deserves active help**, not just waiting. Most experts and guidelines recommend offering treatment to children who are still wetting at this age, especially if they wet frequently or are bothered by it.

This is also the age when children become **much more aware** of bedwetting. They start going to friends' houses, sleepovers come up, school trips begin, and they compare themselves to peers. Feelings of embarrassment and shame can begin here. Your job is both to *treat* the bedwetting and to *protect* your child's confidence.

The good news: children aged 6–8 are often the **ideal age for a bedwetting alarm**. They're old enough to understand and participate, and motivated enough to want to be dry.

## The goals for ages 6–8

1. **See a doctor** for an assessment if you haven't already.
2. **Optimise daytime habits**: drinking, toileting, bowels.
3. **Start active treatment**, usually a bedwetting alarm (or desmopressin if an alarm doesn't suit).
4. **Protect self-esteem** and help your child participate fully in social life.
5. **Involve your child** as an active partner.

## Step 1: Talk to your child

Children of this age often worry silently. Open the conversation gently:

- **Normalise it:** "Lots of kids your age wet the bed. In your class there are probably a couple of others — they just don't talk about it."
- **Explain it simply:** "Your body makes pee at night, and your bladder holds it like a balloon. Your brain is supposed to wake you up when the balloon is full, but your brain is still learning that message. It's not your fault, and it's not because you're lazy."
- **Share family history** if there is one: "Did you know Dad wet the bed until he was 9? It runs in families."
- **Make it a team project:** "We can do some things together to help your body learn. Want to be on the team?"
- **Ask how they feel** and what worries them (sleepovers? friends finding out?).

## Step 2: See the doctor

Book an appointment, ideally after keeping the 2-day bladder diary and 2-week wet-night record described in Chapter 3 and Appendix A. The doctor will:

- Check for constipation, daytime bladder problems, and red flags.
- Test the urine.
- Discuss treatment options with you **and your child**.

## Step 3: Optimise daytime habits (2–4 weeks)

Many children improve with these alone, and they make the alarm or medication more effective.

- **Drinks:** about 1,000–1,400 mL (around 6–8 cups) of water-based drinks a day, most before about 5 p.m. Send a labelled water bottle to school, and agree a target (e.g., "finish and refill once").
- **Stop drinks about an hour before bed.** A small drink with dinner is fine.
- **No caffeine**: cola, iced tea, energy drinks, chocolate drinks.
- **Toilet visits**: on waking, at every school break, after school, before dinner, and twice at bedtime (double void). Teachers can help by reminding your child.
- **Bowels**: daily toilet sit after breakfast or dinner, with feet on a footstool. Look for hard stools, straining, or soiling, and ask your doctor about treatment if needed.
- **School:** ask teachers to allow free access to water and the toilet. Some schools discourage toilet trips in class — a short note from you or your doctor can help.

## Step 4: Start treatment

### Option A: The bedwetting alarm (first choice for most children)

The alarm is especially well suited to this age group. Follow the full instructions in Chapter 2. Key points for 6–8-year-olds:

- **Let your child help choose** the alarm (sound, colour, wearable or mat) — ownership boosts motivation.
- **Practise during the day**, turning it into a fun drill: lie in bed, the alarm "goes off," your child jumps up, switches it off, walks to the toilet, and comes back. Repeat a few times. Rehearsal really does help.
- **Expect to wake your child at first.** Most children this age sleep through the alarm in the early weeks. A parent may need to sleep within earshot (or use a wireless receiver) and wake the child fully — sit them up, call their name, maybe a cool damp cloth on the face — before walking them to the toilet.
- **Keep a progress chart** (Appendix C). Celebrate signs of progress: a smaller wet patch, the alarm going off later in the night, your child waking on their own, waking *before* the alarm.
- **Reward actions**: waking to the alarm, getting up to the toilet, helping reset the bed. Add a special treat for every few stars.
- **Persist.** Review progress after 4 weeks. If improving, keep going until **14 consecutive dry nights**. Typical time is 6–16 weeks.
- **If no improvement after about 4–8 weeks**, talk to your doctor about switching to or adding desmopressin.

### Option B: Desmopressin

Your doctor may suggest desmopressin if:

- an alarm isn't suitable for your family;
- the alarm hasn't worked;
- your child wets very large amounts, often early in the night (suggesting high night-time urine production);
- quick results are needed (e.g., for a school trip).

**Safety first:** no drinks from one hour before the dose until the next morning (8 hours), and stop it if your child is vomiting or has diarrhoea. See the full safety rules in Chapter 2.

### Option C: Combination

If neither works alone, the alarm and desmopressin can be used together, or your doctor may refer you to a specialist continence clinic.

## Step 5: Protect confidence and social life

- **Sleepovers:** don't ban them. Instead, see Chapter 10 for a detailed plan: discreet absorbent pants, a sleeping bag with a liner, desmopressin if prescribed, and a trusted adult at the host home.
- **School trips:** speak to the trip leader confidentially well in advance. Teachers handle this more often than you'd think.
- **Avoid "labels":** don't refer to your child as "a bedwetter." Say "a child who wets the bed" — it's something they *have*, not who they *are*.
- **Siblings:** set a firm family rule — no teasing, ever.
- **Privacy:** don't discuss your child's wetting with relatives or friends without their permission.

## Building independence

Children aged 6–8 can start taking some responsibility — as part of being on the team, not as a penalty:

- Placing wet pyjamas in the laundry basket.
- Helping to strip the bed.
- Getting a fresh pair of pyjamas from their night kit.
- Filling in their own chart.

## ✅ Clear Action Plan: Ages 6–8

**Week 0: Prepare**

- [ ] Have a warm, blame-free conversation with your child; share any family history.
- [ ] Record a 2-day bladder diary and 2 weeks of wet/dry nights (Appendix A).
- [ ] Book a doctor's appointment; bring your records and questions (Appendix E).

**Weeks 1–4: Daytime foundations**

- [ ] 6–8 drinks/day, mostly before 5 p.m.; stop 1 hour before bed; no caffeine.
- [ ] Toilet every 2–3 hours plus a double void at bedtime.
- [ ] Treat constipation if present.
- [ ] Ask school for easy access to water and the toilet.
- [ ] Protect the bed (double-make) and set up a night kit.

**From week 2–4: Active treatment (with your doctor)**

- [ ] **Alarm** (first choice for most): let your child help choose; practise in the day; use every night; parent wakes the child at first; record progress; continue until 14 dry nights in a row.
- [ ] Review at 4 weeks — continue if improving; see your doctor if not.
- [ ] **Or desmopressin** if the alarm isn't suitable — follow fluid rules strictly.

**Always**

- [ ] Reward actions, not dry nights.
- [ ] No punishment, shame, or teasing.
- [ ] Plan sleepovers and trips rather than avoiding them (Chapter 10).
- [ ] See a doctor promptly for any red flags (Chapter 3).
