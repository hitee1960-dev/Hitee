# Ages 9–12: Taking Action Before the Teen Years

## What's happening at this age

Around **5% of 10-year-olds** still wet the bed — still one or two children in most classes. By this age, bedwetting is much less likely to resolve quickly on its own, especially in children who wet most nights, and its emotional impact tends to grow. Research has linked bedwetting that continues past age 10 with lower self-esteem and more emotional and behavioural difficulties in early adolescence — though these usually *improve* when the wetting is successfully treated.

This is the age of **residential school trips, camps, sleepovers, and sports tournaments**, and the age when children become intensely sensitive about fitting in. The message for parents is clear: **if your child is still wetting at 9–12, now is the time to treat it actively and persistently.** Don't wait for the teenage years.

The other big shift: your child can now be a genuine **partner and leader** in their own treatment.

## The goals for ages 9–12

1. **Get a proper medical assessment** — and a referral to a specialist continence service if first-line treatments haven't worked.
2. **Put your child in the driver's seat**, with your support.
3. **Use the alarm and/or desmopressin consistently** — and combinations if needed.
4. **Plan for social events** so bedwetting never stops your child from taking part.
5. **Watch and protect emotional wellbeing.**

## Step 1: Hand over ownership (with backup)

Children this age respond best when they are treated as the expert on their own body.

- **Explain the science** properly: vasopressin (the "night-time hormone"), bladder capacity, and the bladder-brain wake-up signal (Chapter 1). Many children are relieved to learn there's a physical explanation.
- **Let them read parts of this book** — Chapters 1 and 2 are written so a 10–12-year-old can follow them.
- **Agree a plan together** and write it down. Ask: "What do *you* want to try? What would help most?"
- **Give them control** over their chart, their water-bottle target, and their night kit.
- **Stay involved:** your role shifts from "doing" to "supporting" — especially with the alarm, where they may still need you to wake them.

## Step 2: See the doctor (again, if needed)

If your child has never been assessed, book an appointment now, bringing a bladder diary and wet-night record (Appendix A). If your child has *already* tried treatment:

- Ask what went wrong last time. Was the alarm used every night for long enough (at least 6–8 weeks)? Was constipation treated? Was the dose of desmopressin optimised and taken correctly?
- Ask about **combination treatment** or a **referral to a specialist** (pediatric continence clinic, pediatric urologist, or nephrologist) if two good attempts at first-line treatment have failed. NICE recommends referral when children don't respond to two complete courses of either alarm or desmopressin.
- Raise any **daytime symptoms, constipation, or snoring** again — these are common hidden reasons for treatment failure.

## Step 3: Daytime foundations

The basics still matter enormously:

- **Drinks:** about 1,200–2,100 mL for girls and 1,400–2,300 mL for boys (roughly 6–10 cups), spread through the day, most before about 5 p.m. Pre-teens often drink very little at school — **this is a common cause of poor results**. Agree a specific school-day target (e.g., two full bottles).
- **Cut caffeine:** many pre-teens start drinking cola, iced tea, or energy drinks. These can significantly worsen bedwetting. Energy drinks should be avoided entirely.
- **Toilet routine:** on waking, at school breaks, after school, before dinner, and twice at bedtime.
- **Bowels:** check for constipation honestly — pre-teens may be embarrassed to discuss it. Use the Bristol Stool Chart as a neutral tool.
- **Screens and sleep:** a regular bedtime and screens off 30–60 minutes before sleep support healthy sleep patterns.

## Step 4: Active treatment

### The alarm

The alarm remains the best long-term option for many children at this age.

- **Choose a model your child likes** — many pre-teens prefer discreet wireless or vibrating alarms.
- **Your child should be the one to respond** to the alarm whenever possible: get up, switch it off, finish at the toilet, change, reset. If they sleep through it, a parent still needs to wake them, and that's okay.
- **Track progress** using the alarm log (Appendix C). Many 9–12-year-olds love seeing the data improve.
- **Commit:** nightly use, review after 4 weeks, continue until **14 dry nights in a row**. Consider "overlearning" (Chapter 2) to reduce relapse.

### Desmopressin

Desmopressin is often extremely useful at this age because of:

- **School trips and camps**: it can be started a few weeks before an event to check it works and find the right dose, then used during the trip.
- **High night-time urine output**: common in children who wet large volumes early in the night.
- **Families who can't manage an alarm** at this stage.

Your child should understand and follow the **fluid rules** themselves — no drinks from one hour before the dose until the next morning — and know to tell an adult if they get a headache or feel sick. Remember to stop it during vomiting or diarrhoea illness.

### Combination and specialist options

If first-line treatments don't work, specialists may use alarm plus desmopressin, add an anticholinergic medicine when an overactive bladder is suspected, or arrange further tests. Don't give up — the vast majority of children eventually become dry.

## Step 5: Social life — plan, don't avoid

**Never let bedwetting become a reason to miss out.** See Chapter 10 for the full sleepover and school-trip plan. Key points:

- **Trial desmopressin** at home several weeks before a residential trip.
- **Discreet absorbent pants** designed for older children are slim and look like regular underwear.
- **Talk confidentially to trip staff** in advance; ask how they'll handle it discreetly (e.g., a teacher quietly waking your child to use the toilet, a private place to change, a bag for wet items).
- **Practice runs:** a sleepover at a relative's house can build confidence.
- **Agree a cover story** with your child, such as keeping their night pants in a toilet bag and changing in the bathroom.

## Step 6: Emotional wellbeing

Watch for signs that bedwetting is weighing heavily:

- Withdrawal, refusal of invitations, avoiding friends.
- Low mood, irritability, tearfulness, or saying "I'm stupid," "I'm a baby."
- Anxiety at bedtime or trouble getting to sleep.
- Being teased or bullied.

If you see these signs, talk to your child and to your doctor — counselling or psychological support can help alongside treatment. See Chapter 11.

# Ages 13–16: Supporting Teens with Privacy and Respect

> **For teens reading this chapter:** This chapter is written for you as much as for your parents. You're not alone — around 1–3 in every 100 teenagers still wet the bed, which means at a school of 1,000 students, there could be 10–30 others going through the same thing. It is a medical condition with physical causes, not something you're doing wrong. And it can be treated.

## What's happening at this age

By the teenage years, bedwetting is **less common, but often more persistent and more distressing**. Teens who still wet usually do so for physical reasons: high night-time urine production, a bladder that holds less overnight, difficulty waking — or a combination. Studies suggest that teenage bedwetting is more likely to be frequent and less likely to stop on its own than childhood bedwetting, so **active treatment is strongly recommended**.

Puberty brings growth spurts and hormonal changes. In some teens, this coincides with improvement; in others, it doesn't. Waiting and hoping is not a plan.

Teenagers also face unique pressures: intense need for privacy, fear of friends finding out, relationships, overnight trips, sports camps, and growing independence. Bedwetting can affect self-image, mood, sleep, and social life. Teens with bedwetting are more likely to report feeling ashamed, avoiding social events, and feeling "different" — but successful treatment is associated with real improvements in self-esteem.

## The goals for ages 13–16

1. **Respect privacy and autonomy** — the teen leads, the parent supports.
2. **Get a thorough medical assessment**, including checks for less common causes.
3. **Use effective treatment** — often desmopressin, the alarm, or both — and specialist help if needed.
4. **Support a full social life.**
5. **Protect mental health.**

## For parents: shifting your role

- **Ask, don't tell.** "How are you feeling about the wetting? Do you want help with it? What would help?"
- **Respect privacy absolutely.** Never mention it in front of siblings, relatives, or friends. Knock before entering. Keep supplies discreet.
- **Offer, don't push**, a doctor's visit. Many teens welcome it once they know it's treatable. Offer that they can see the doctor alone for part or all of the visit.
- **Make practicalities invisible.** Stock supplies without comment; let your teen manage their own laundry if they prefer; provide a lidded laundry bin or wash bag in their room.
- **Stay calm and positive** about setbacks.
- **Watch for signs of distress**: withdrawal, refusing invitations, low mood, anxiety about sleeping away, or difficulty sleeping.

## For teens: your action steps

### 1. Understand your body

Read Chapters 1 and 2 of this book. Knowing *why* it happens — night-time hormone levels, bladder capacity, and waking signals — makes it easier to fix and helps you see it's not your fault.

### 2. See a doctor

You can ask your parent to book the appointment, or (depending on where you live and your age) book it yourself. You have the right to speak to the doctor privately. Before you go:

- **Keep a 2-day bladder diary** (Appendix A) — what you drink, when you pee and how much, and any accidents.
- **Record wet and dry nights for 2 weeks.**
- **Note other symptoms**: needing to pee urgently or often in the day, daytime leaks, constipation, snoring, excessive thirst, weight changes.
- **Write down your questions** (Appendix E).

The doctor should test your urine and examine you. For teenagers, doctors are more likely to look carefully for underlying causes, especially if wetting has started again after being dry, if there are daytime symptoms, or if treatment hasn't worked before. They may refer you to a specialist.

### 3. Fix the daytime basics

These make a real difference, especially in teens:

- **Drink well during the day:** around 1,400–2,500 mL for girls and 2,100–3,200 mL for boys (roughly 6–13 cups, more if you're very active or it's hot). Carry a water bottle and drink most of it before late afternoon.
- **Stop drinks about an hour before bed.** If you're thirsty late in the evening, it's usually a sign you didn't drink enough during the day.
- **Cut out caffeine and energy drinks**, especially after midday: coffee, cola, energy drinks, pre-workout drinks, and strong tea all increase urine production and irritate the bladder.
- **Avoid alcohol.** Alcohol strongly increases night-time urine and makes waking harder (and is illegal for under-18s in many countries).
- **Go to the toilet regularly** during the day — don't "hold it" all day at school.
- **Keep your bowels regular** — constipation is common in teens (especially with fast food, low fibre, and avoiding school toilets) and directly affects the bladder.
- **Sleep well:** regular bedtimes and phone/screens off before sleep. Very late nights and sleep deprivation make the brain harder to wake.
- **Pee twice before bed** — once when you start getting ready and again just before you fall asleep.

### 4. Choose a treatment with your doctor

**Desmopressin** is often a good fit for teens:

- It works fast and is easy to take discreetly (tablets or melts).
- It's useful for overnight trips, camps, and staying at a partner's or friend's house.
- **Follow the fluid rules strictly**: no more than small sips from 1 hour before taking it until 8 hours after. This is especially important at parties, sports events, or in hot weather. **Never take it after drinking alcohol or a lot of fluid.**
- Skip it if you're vomiting or have diarrhoea, and get medical help if you develop a headache, nausea, or confusion while taking it.

**The bedwetting alarm** is still highly effective for teens and is often a longer-lasting fix:

- **Vibrating or wireless alarms** are more discreet and won't wake the whole house.
- You're more likely to wake up to it yourself than a younger child — but if not, decide with your parent whether they'll help wake you during the first weeks.
- Use it every night for at least 6–8 weeks; continue until 14 dry nights in a row.

**Combination or specialist treatment:** if one treatment doesn't work, don't give up. Alarm plus desmopressin, adding a bladder-relaxing medicine, or specialist bladder training may be recommended. Ask for a referral to a specialist continence clinic if two full treatment courses haven't helped.

### 5. Manage the practical side with confidence

- **Discreet protection:** modern teen/young-adult absorbent underwear is slim and looks like regular underwear. Washable protective pants are another option.
- **Mattress protection:** a quiet, breathable waterproof protector won't crinkle.
- **Your own night kit:** spare underwear, pyjamas, wipes, a towel, and a sealable wash bag — kept somewhere private.
- **Take control of laundry** if it helps your privacy — rinse and wash wet items in the morning.
- **Shower in the morning** to stay fresh and confident.

### 6. Sleepovers, camps, and trips

See Chapter 10 for a full plan. Key moves for teens:

- **Trial desmopressin** at home before the event.
- **Pack discreetly:** night pants in a toiletries bag; change in the bathroom.
- **Sleeping bag strategy:** a sleeping bag with a waterproof liner or an absorbent pad inside can hide accidents.
- **Tell one trusted adult** (a coach, teacher, or camp nurse) — they handle this confidentially and more often than you'd imagine.
- **Decide what you'll say** if anything happens: you don't owe anyone an explanation. "I spilled my water" is a classic for a reason.

### 7. Look after your mental health

It's normal to feel embarrassed, frustrated, or fed up. But if you notice you're:

- avoiding friends, sports, or trips you used to enjoy,
- feeling down, anxious, or hopeless most days,
- being teased or bullied,
- having thoughts of hurting yourself,

please talk to someone you trust — a parent, doctor, school counsellor, or a mental health helpline. **If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, seek help immediately** from emergency services or a crisis line in your country.

# Ages 16–19: Moving Toward Independence

> **For young people reading this chapter:** If you're 16–19 and still wetting at night, you might feel like you're the only one. You're not. Studies estimate that around 1–2% of older teens and adults experience bedwetting — that's millions of people worldwide. It's a medical condition, it can be investigated and treated, and you deserve good care for it.

## What's happening at this age

By the late teens, **bedwetting that has persisted since childhood is less likely to resolve on its own** without treatment, and wetting that starts *new* at this age (secondary enuresis) always needs a medical check. Young people at this stage are also going through major life changes: finishing school, jobs, driving, first relationships, travel, college or university, and possibly moving out of home into shared accommodation. These transitions can make bedwetting feel especially daunting — but they also make it a perfect time to take decisive action.

## The goals for ages 16–19

1. **Take ownership** of your own health care.
2. **Get a thorough assessment** — ideally including specialist review if earlier treatment has failed.
3. **Find a treatment strategy that fits adult life.**
4. **Plan confidently** for college, travel, relationships, and independent living.
5. **Protect your mental wellbeing.**

## Step 1: Take charge of your medical care

At this age, you can usually book and attend appointments on your own and speak to a doctor confidentially.

- **Book an appointment** with your family doctor or primary care provider. Say clearly: "I've had bedwetting [since childhood / since a certain time] and I'd like help with it."
- **Bring data**: a 2–3 day bladder diary (Appendix A), 2 weeks of wet/dry nights, and a list of previous treatments and how they went.
- **Mention any other symptoms**: daytime urgency or leaks, frequent peeing, weak stream, constipation, snoring or breathing pauses, excessive thirst, weight loss, back pain or leg symptoms, anxiety or low mood.
- **Expect a thorough check**: urine test, examination, and possibly further tests such as blood tests, a kidney and bladder ultrasound, a test measuring urine flow and how well the bladder empties, or a sleep study if sleep-disordered breathing is suspected. Persistent bedwetting at this age warrants a careful look for treatable causes such as an overactive bladder, a bladder that doesn't empty fully, or obstructed breathing during sleep.
- **Ask for a specialist referral** (urologist, nephrologist, or specialist continence service) if first-line treatments haven't worked.

## Step 2: Plan for the transition from pediatric to adult care

If you've been seen by a children's specialist, ask them about **transition**: being handed over to an adult service in good time, rather than being discharged with nowhere to go. Ask for:

- A written summary of your diagnosis, tests, and treatments so far.
- A named adult service or contact.
- Your current prescriptions and how to renew them.

## Step 3: Lifestyle factors that matter more at this age

- **Fluids:** aim for around 1,400–2,500 mL (women) or 2,100–3,200 mL (men) through the day, most before late afternoon. Stop drinking about an hour before bed.
- **Caffeine:** coffee, energy drinks, and pre-workout supplements are common at this age and can substantially worsen bedwetting — avoid them after midday or altogether.
- **Alcohol:** alcohol suppresses the hormone that reduces night-time urine, increases urine volume, and makes you harder to wake. It's one of the biggest triggers for wetting in young adults. If you drink (and are of legal age), limit it, avoid it on nights you want to stay dry, and **never combine it with desmopressin**.
- **Cannabis and other substances** can deepen sleep and affect bladder control — another reason to avoid them.
- **Sleep:** irregular schedules, all-nighters, and sleep debt make the brain harder to wake. Keep a reasonably regular sleep pattern.
- **Bowels:** student diets low in fibre and high in fast food lead to constipation. Keep fibre and fluids up.
- **Weight and exercise:** regular physical activity supports bowel and bladder health. Pelvic floor exercises taught by a physiotherapist may help some people with daytime symptoms.

## Step 4: Treatment options for young adults

- **Desmopressin:** often the most practical option for independent life, travel, and shared living. It requires disciplined fluid rules (no drinks from 1 hour before to 8 hours after) and **must not be taken after drinking alcohol or large volumes of fluid**. Note that safety precautions are similar for adults; your doctor will review the dose and formulation.
- **Bedwetting alarm:** still effective at this age. Vibrating or wireless alarms suit shared housing and dorms. If you live alone and sleep through alarms, a pad alarm with a loud bedside unit, a vibrating pillow pad, or a light plus sound combination may help.
- **Combination therapy** (desmopressin + alarm, or desmopressin + a bladder-relaxing medicine) when one treatment isn't enough.
- **Specialist treatments** for an overactive bladder or incomplete bladder emptying, if these are found.
- **Treatment of sleep-disordered breathing**, if identified.
- **Psychological support** — especially cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) — to manage anxiety, shame, and the impact on your life, alongside medical treatment.

## Step 5: Living with confidence

### College, university, and shared housing

- **Request a single room** if possible. Many colleges will accommodate medical needs confidentially — a letter from your doctor may help. You don't have to disclose the specific condition to housing staff in detail.
- **Disability or student support services** can arrange accommodations confidentially.
- **Set up your room:** quiet waterproof mattress protector, layered bedding, a lidded laundry bag, and a discreet supply of protection.
- **Laundry plan:** a portable laundry bag, a schedule that suits you, and spare sheets.

### Travel and holidays

- **Pack a travel kit:** absorbent underwear, a portable waterproof sheet or travel bed pad, disposable bags, wipes, a change of clothes.
- **Carry your medication in hand luggage** with a copy of the prescription.
- **Time zones:** take desmopressin at your local bedtime and follow fluid rules; discuss long-haul travel with your doctor.
- **Hotels and hostels:** a waterproof travel sheet protects the mattress and avoids awkward conversations.

### Relationships

- **You decide** if, when, and how to tell a partner. There's no obligation to disclose early in a relationship.
- Many people find that when they do share it, partners are understanding — especially when it's explained as a medical condition being treated.
- A simple script: "I want to tell you something personal. I have a medical condition that sometimes causes me to wet the bed. It's being treated, and I manage it with [protection/medication]. I wanted you to know."
- Plan practicalities for overnight stays: desmopressin, discreet protection, or a waterproof pad in your bag.

### Work

- Bedwetting rarely affects work directly, but shift work, travel, and work trips can raise practical issues. Use the same planning approach as for trips.

## Step 6: Protecting your mental health

Living with bedwetting as a young adult can bring shame, anxiety around intimacy and travel, and avoidance of opportunities. You deserve support:

- Talk to your doctor about how it's affecting you — psychological impact is a valid reason to escalate treatment.
- Consider counselling or CBT.
- Connect with **reputable continence charities and patient organisations** in your country — many run helplines and online information for young people.
- **If you're feeling hopeless or having thoughts of self-harm**, contact emergency services or a crisis line in your country immediately.
