## Appendix E: Doctor's Visit Checklist and Questions {.unnumbered}

**Bring with you:**

- [ ] Two-day bladder diary
- [ ] Two-week wet-night record
- [ ] One-week bowel record
- [ ] List of other symptoms (daytime wetting, urgency, snoring, thirst, mood)
- [ ] Family history of bedwetting
- [ ] Treatments already tried, how long, and results
- [ ] Current medicines

**Questions to ask:**

1. What type of bedwetting does my child have (primary/secondary, with or without daytime symptoms)?
2. Is there anything that needs testing or investigating?
3. Is my child constipated? How should we treat it?
4. Would an alarm or desmopressin suit my child better — and why?
5. How long should we try the treatment before reviewing it?
6. Can we borrow an alarm through a local service?
7. If desmopressin: what are the fluid rules, when should we stop it, and what warning signs should we watch for?
8. What should we do if the treatment doesn't work?
9. When would you refer us to a specialist?
10. Is there support available for my child's feelings or for our family?

## Appendix F: Quick Reference — Actions by Age {.unnumbered}

| Age | Is wetting normal? | Main focus | Key actions | See a doctor if... |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0–2** | Yes — fully normal | Healthy foundations | Water through the day; fibre; prevent constipation; relaxed daytime potty training when ready; keep night diapers | Constipation, constant dribbling, weak stream, unexplained fevers, excessive thirst, loud snoring |
| **3–5** | Yes — common (about 1 in 5 at age 5) | Readiness and habits | Watch for dry mornings; try nights without pants when ready; 6–8 drinks/day; regular toilet visits; double void; calm responses; sticker charts for actions | Age 5+ and bothered by it; daytime symptoms; constipation; wetting after 6 months dry; red flags |
| **6–8** | Common (7–10%) but now treatable | Active treatment begins | Doctor's assessment; optimise drinks/toilet/bowels; **alarm** first-line (or desmopressin); reward effort; plan sleepovers | Treatment recommended — book an assessment |
| **9–12** | Less common (about 5%) | Persistent, child-led treatment | Child leads the plan; school water target; no caffeine; alarm and/or desmopressin; trial medication before trips; watch mood | Not responding after 2 treatment courses → specialist |
| **13–16** | Uncommon (1–3%) | Privacy, autonomy, effective treatment | Private consultations; strict caffeine/alcohol avoidance; desmopressin and/or discreet alarm; private night kit; trip planning; mental health | Always worth assessment; specialist if treatment fails; any red flags |
| **16–19** | Uncommon (1–2%) | Independence and adult care | Own appointments; thorough tests; transition to adult services; alcohol awareness; college/travel/relationship planning; CBT/support | Always; new-onset wetting needs prompt assessment |

# Glossary {.unnumbered}

**Alarm (enuresis alarm):** A device that sounds or vibrates when it detects moisture, used to train a child to wake when the bladder is full.

**Anticholinergic:** A type of medicine (e.g., oxybutynin) that relaxes an overactive bladder.

**Bladder capacity:** The amount of urine the bladder can comfortably hold.

**Bladder diary (frequency-volume chart):** A record of drinks, urine volumes, and accidents.

**Bristol Stool Chart:** A seven-point scale describing stool types, used to assess constipation.

**Constipation:** Infrequent, hard, or painful bowel movements, or incomplete emptying.

**Desmopressin:** A synthetic version of vasopressin that reduces night-time urine production.

**Double voiding:** Going to the toilet twice in quick succession (e.g., at the start and end of the bedtime routine) to empty the bladder fully.

**Enuresis (nocturnal):** Wetting during sleep in a child aged 5 or older.

**Hyponatremia:** A low level of sodium in the blood, a rare but serious risk if a person drinks too much while taking desmopressin.

**Lifting:** Carrying or waking a child to the toilet during the night.

**Monosymptomatic enuresis:** Night-time wetting without daytime bladder symptoms.

**Nocturnal polyuria:** Producing an unusually large amount of urine at night.

**Non-monosymptomatic enuresis:** Night-time wetting with daytime bladder symptoms.

**Overactive bladder:** A bladder that contracts suddenly and frequently, causing urgency and frequent urination.

**Overlearning:** Continuing alarm treatment with an extra evening drink after initial success, to reduce relapse.

**Primary enuresis:** Bedwetting in a child who has never been consistently dry at night.

**Secondary enuresis:** Bedwetting that returns after at least six months of dryness.

**Vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone, ADH):** A natural hormone that tells the kidneys to make less urine.
